Most people spend years waiting for leadership to find them.

They wait for the title, the promotion, the moment someone finally says, "Now you're ready." But the most consistent message in leadership research runs the other way: leadership is not something you are given. It is something you practise, starting long before anyone puts your name at the top of an org chart.

What follows are nine principles, grouped into three pillars: leading yourself, leading with others, and making a difference. Each comes with one action you can take this week.

Pillar 1: Leading yourself

1. Start before the title.

Robert Greenleaf (1970) described the servant-leader as someone who is a servant first: the choice to take responsibility for others comes before the position, not after it. Leadership shows up in the conversation nobody else is willing to have, and in the moment everyone steps back and you don't.

This week: take ownership of one problem that isn't formally yours.

2. Know yourself before you lead anyone else.

The people who grow fastest are rarely the most talented. They are the most self-aware. Daniel Goleman (1998) places self-awareness at the foundation of emotional intelligence: knowing your triggers before they derail a conversation, and your blind spots before they become team problems.

This week: ask one colleague, "What's one thing I do that gets in the way?" Then just listen.

3. Move from managing to leading.

Managing asks, "Is this done?" Leading asks, "What do you need from me?" Amy Edmondson's (1999) research on teams found that learning and performance depend heavily on psychological safety: whether people feel safe to speak up, admit mistakes and ask for help. That safety is built by leaders, one question at a time.

This week: in your next one-to-one, ask "What do you actually need from me right now?" before any status update.

Pillar 2: Leading with others

4. Get close, not distant.

Some leaders lead from behind glass: they always know the numbers and rarely know their people. Peters and Waterman (1982) found that excellent companies were run by leaders who stayed close to the work and the people doing it, an approach often called "management by walking around". Presence says what no dashboard can: this matters, and so do you.

The line between presence and micromanagement is intent. Micromanagement says, "I don't trust you." Presence says, "I care enough to show up."

This week: spend 30 minutes where the work actually happens, and ask questions instead of giving answers.

5. Do the work nobody sees.

The most important things a leader does are often invisible: the closed-door conversation fighting for someone, the pushback on a decision that wasn't fair to the team. Bill George (2003) describes authentic leaders as the same person in private as in public. Teams can't see these moments, but they feel them, in how safe they are to take risks and how far they'll go for you.

This week: speak up once, in a room your team isn't in, for something they need.

6. Treat hard seasons as adaptive work.

Ronald Heifetz and Donald Laurie (1997) distinguish technical problems, which have known solutions, from adaptive challenges, which require people to change their assumptions and habits. The hardest seasons in leadership are almost always adaptive: no playbook, pressure from above, morale slipping below. Leaders who come through them stronger don't manage the pressure from a distance. They move closer and ask harder questions.

This week: name one challenge on your plate that is adaptive, not technical, and stop trying to solve it alone.

Pillar 3: Making a difference

7. Build influence, not just authority.

"Because I'm the manager" works, technically. People comply. But there is a ceiling to what authority alone can build. French and Raven (1959) separated power that comes from position from power that comes from respect, and Goffee and Jones (2000) found that people choose to follow leaders who are authentic and genuinely invested in them. Compliance shows up when someone is watching. Commitment shows up when no one is.

This week: replace one "because I said so" with the real reason behind the decision.

8. Grow people who outgrow you.

In his study of companies that went from good to great, Jim Collins (2001) found that their leaders combined personal humility with fierce professional will. They channelled their ambition into the organization and its people, not into themselves, and were proud, not threatened, when their people outgrew them.

This week: hand one visible opportunity to someone on your team, and give them the credit publicly.

9. Don't wait to feel ready.

Carol Dweck's (2006) research on growth mindset shows that abilities are developed through effort and learning, not fixed at birth. Readiness does not come before the leap. It comes because of it. The people who grow most are not the most gifted; they are the most willing to step in before they feel certain and to be visibly imperfect while they learn.

This week: say yes to one stretch opportunity you would normally wait on.

The bottom line

Leadership is not a destination you arrive at. It is a practice you choose every day: in small decisions, in hard conversations, and in the moments when stepping back would be easier than stepping forward.

You don't need permission. You need a decision.

Over to you: which of the nine are you practising well right now, and which one needs work?
