Bridging the Gap, edition 3

Why Good Leaders Misread People. How to Stop?

Personality tells you how people tend to act. Perception decides how you read them. Most leadership friction lives in the gap between the two.

Hossam AlmotaimOctober 8, 2026, 7 min read

Every leader has had this moment.

A capable team member misses a deadline. You feel the judgement form before you've asked a single question: careless. A week later you miss one yourself, and the explanation comes just as quickly: the workload was impossible.

Same event. Two completely different stories.

That gap between what happened and what we believe happened is where much of the friction in organizations begins. It isn't usually a strategy problem or a skills problem. It's a reading problem: how we read other people, and how accurately we read ourselves.

Two ideas from organizational behaviour explain most of it: personality and perception. Understood together, they are among the most practical tools a leader can have.

1. Personality isn't a label. It's a set of if–thens.

We often talk about personality as if it were a fixed badge: she's an introvert, he's a Type A. The research paints a more useful picture.

Mischel and Shoda (1995) describe personality as a system of "if–then" patterns. If the deadline is tight, then I become sharp. If the client is upset, then I become calm. The person hasn't changed; the situation has triggered a different part of them.

This is why no trait is simply good or bad. Tett and Burnett (2003) show that a trait only helps or hurts when the situation activates it. The decisive leader who shines in a crisis can flatten a brainstorm. The careful analyst who saves a project can stall its launch.

And the people who read those situations well tend to go further. Kilduff and Day (1994) followed 139 MBA graduates for five years. High self-monitors, people who adjust their behaviour to the situation, were more likely to be promoted, both within their company and across companies.

2. The trait most models miss

The Big Five (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and emotional stability) has long been the standard framework for personality at work. Conscientiousness in particular is one of the most consistent predictors of job performance (Barrick & Mount, 1991).

The HEXACO model adds a sixth factor: Honesty-Humility, which covers sincerity, fairness, modesty and a lack of greed. Ashton and Lee (2007) argue it captures behaviour the Big Five can miss, including integrity-related conduct at work.

For leaders, this matters most in hiring. The most confident, charming candidate is not always the safest one. Research on the "Dark Triad" of narcissism, Machiavellianism and psychopathy shows these traits can come with considerable surface charm (Paulhus & Williams, 2002). Hire for character, not only charisma.

3. We don't react to reality. We react to what we perceive.

Perception is how we select, organise and interpret information. In every meeting, email and performance review, it quietly decides what people believe happened.

Three patterns shape it more than most leaders realise:

  • Attribution. We explain other people's failures by their character and our own by our circumstances. Ross (1977) called the first half of this the fundamental attribution error.
  • Social identity. We see people in our own group more favourably, and judge the same behaviour from an "outsider" more harshly (Tajfel & Turner, 1979).
  • Expectations. In a well-known field study, instructors were told that certain trainees, who had in fact been chosen at random, had high potential. Those trainees went on to perform better (Eden & Shani, 1982). A leader's expectations don't just describe a team; they help shape it.

4. Where personality and perception meet: the blind spot

Here is how the two ideas connect. Your personality shapes what you notice. What you notice shapes how you behave. And your behaviour is the only thing your team actually sees.

The Johari Window (Luft & Ingham, 1955) makes this visible. It divides what is known about you into four panes: open, hidden, blind and unknown. The blind spot is what others see in you that you cannot see yourself, and it is where much leadership friction lives.

Self-awareness is rarer than we assume. Eurich (2018) reports that while most people believe they are self-aware, only a small minority actually are. The gap closes in only one way: through feedback.

A practical playbook for leaders

  1. Treat personality tests as a mirror, not a verdict. Self-reports run high on good days and low on bad ones. Use results to start a conversation, never to end one.
  2. Learn your own if–thens. Write down the three situations that bring out your best and the three that bring out your worst. Then plan for the second list.
  3. Hire for character, not only charisma. Add interview questions that reveal fairness and humility, not just confidence.
  4. Ask "what else could explain this?" before you judge. Separate the person from the situation every time performance slips.
  5. Set high expectations out loud. People tend to rise, or sink, to what they believe you expect of them.
  6. Ask for feedback on your blind spot, and thank the person who gives it. The first honest answer is the hardest to get. Make it safe to give.

The bottom line

Personality is where you start. Self-awareness is where you end up.

Perception isn't reality, but it is the reality your team acts on. The leaders who build the strongest teams aren't the ones with the "right" personality. They're the ones who know how they come across, and who keep checking whether the story in their head matches what actually happened.

Over to you: when did a first impression, yours or someone else's, turn out to be completely wrong?
