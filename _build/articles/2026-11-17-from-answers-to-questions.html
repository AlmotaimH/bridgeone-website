Most managers are promoted for being good at answers. Then the answers become the job. Every problem walks to your desk, every meeting waits for your view, and every decision slows to the speed of your calendar.

It feels like leadership. It is often a bottleneck with a title.

In the last two posts, we looked at two sides of the same habit. Coaching's biggest payoff is people who can steer themselves. Idea generation works better when people think alone before they think together. This edition connects them, and adds the step most leaders skip: deciding which kind of problem is on the table before choosing how to respond.

1. The double diamond of questions

Good coaching and good problem-solving share the same shape. They open up, then they focus. Twice.

The classic coaching model, GROW, popularized by John Whitmore's Coaching for Performance in 1992, moves from Goal to Reality to Options to Will (Whitmore, 2017). Creative problem-solving alternates divergent thinking, which generates many possibilities, with convergent thinking, which narrows them to the best one (Guilford, 1956; Osborn, 1953). Designers know this rhythm as the Double Diamond, which the Design Council introduced in 2005 and expanded in 2019 (Design Council, 2019).

Put them together and you get a simple map. The first diamond is coaching: open up the person and the situation, then focus on the real problem. The second diamond is creative problem-solving: open up the options, then commit to one step. Between the two diamonds sits the moment that matters most: the real question.

• Goal: What do you want from this?

• Reality: What is really happening, and who sees it differently?

• Options: What else could work?

• Will: What will you do first, and by when?

2. Step zero: which kind of problem is this?

Not every problem deserves a coaching conversation. Heifetz and Laurie (1997) separate technical problems, where the answer is known and an expert can apply it, from adaptive challenges, where there is no ready answer and the people involved have to learn and change.

The rule is simple. If it is technical, give the answer, fast. Coaching someone through a password reset wastes everyone's time. If it is adaptive, ask, and give the work back to the people who will have to live with the solution. Heifetz and Laurie argue that the solutions to adaptive challenges sit in the collective intelligence of people throughout the organization, not in the executive suite.

The most common leadership mistake is treating an adaptive challenge as a technical one: announcing a fix for a problem that needed people to change how they work. The fix fails, and the problem returns.

3. Diamond one: find the real problem

Most organizations are not bad at solving problems. They are bad at choosing which problem to solve. In a survey of 106 C-suite executives from 91 companies in 17 countries, 85% agreed that their organizations were bad at problem diagnosis, and 87% agreed that this flaw carried significant costs (Wedell-Wedellsborg, 2017). The research points the same way: a meta-analysis of 40 studies found that people who are better at finding and framing problems also tend to score higher on creativity (Abdulla et al., 2020).

The remedy is reframing. Wedell-Wedellsborg tells the story of tenants complaining about a slow elevator. The obvious answers were expensive: a faster motor, a new lift. The reframe was cheap: the problem was not the speed but the boring wait, and a mirror beside the doors made the complaints drop.

Three questions open the first diamond:

• What is the problem behind the problem?

• Who would describe this differently, and how?

• When has this worked well, and what was different then? This is the appreciative-inquiry question: start from what already works, not only from what is broken (Cooperrider & Whitney, 2005).

Notice that every one of them is a question. A leader who answers too fast skips the first diamond entirely and solves the first problem that was named, which is rarely the real one.

4. Diamond two: grow the options, then commit

Once the real problem is clear, open up again. Generate before you judge (Osborn, 1953). Give people quiet time to think alone before the discussion, so the first confident voice does not set the direction for everyone else. Ask for quantity first, quality second.

Then focus. Agree on two or three criteria before you compare ideas, so the choice is about the problem and not about whose idea it was. Pick one option to test small rather than one plan to roll out big.

Finally, close the diamond with Will. Every coaching conversation and every problem-solving meeting should end with a named owner, a first step and a date. If that owner is always you, the conversation was not coaching. It was delegation in reverse.

5. Myth vs evidence

• Myth: Good leaders have the answers. Evidence: for adaptive challenges, the answers sit with the people doing the work; the leader's job is to ask and to give the work back (Heifetz & Laurie, 1997).

• Myth: Coaching means never giving advice. Evidence: technical problems need answers; adaptive ones need the people involved to do the work (Heifetz & Laurie, 1997). Coaching is a choice you make once you know what kind of problem you face.

• Myth: Creativity can't be trained. Evidence: a review of 169 training studies found an average effect of about half a standard deviation, which shrank to about 0.3 after correcting for publication bias, and the authors warn that study quality is weak (Sio & Lortie-Forgues, 2024). Training helps, but less than often claimed. Practise it; don't buy miracles.

• Myth: Fast fixes show strong leadership. Evidence: speed on the wrong problem is waste. Executives themselves rate poor diagnosis as costly (Wedell-Wedellsborg, 2017).

6. The two-minute team check-up

Answer yes or no. Every "no" shows you where to start.

1. I ask before I advise.

2. People bring me options, not only problems.

3. We define the problem before we solve it.

4. Every voice is heard before we choose.

5. We separate generating ideas from judging them.

6. I know which of our problems are adaptive.

7. Each meeting ends with a named owner and a date.

8. People solve more without me than they did a month ago.

7. The 30-day playbook

• Week 1, Pause: in your one-to-ones, count how often you answer and how often you ask. Don't change anything yet. Just notice.

• Week 2, Reframe: before your next decision meeting, ask "What's the problem behind the problem?" and rewrite it as a "How might we...?" question.

• Week 3, Diverge: run one silent idea round, five minutes of writing alone, before any debate.

• Week 4, Hand back: close every coaching conversation with an owner, a first step and a date, and make sure the owner isn't you.

The bottom line

Answers end conversations. Questions grow leaders. The skill is not choosing one over the other. It is knowing which problem you are facing, and having the discipline to ask before you answer when the problem belongs to your people.
