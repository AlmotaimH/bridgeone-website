Bridging the Gap, edition 4

Why People Stop Trying, and How Leaders Bring Them Back

Motivation and learning aren't two separate topics. A useful way to understand their relationship is as a loop, and leaders can unintentionally disrupt it.

Hossam AlmotaimOctober 24, 2026, 9 min read

Every leader has seen it. Someone capable, who used to volunteer for everything, now does exactly what's asked, and not one thing more.

The easy diagnosis is "they've lost motivation." The more useful question is: what broke?

In my last two posts, I looked at motivation (why pay alone may not sustain effort) and feedback (why some feedback interventions can backfire). This edition connects them through a practical model: learning can strengthen confidence and effort, while poor feedback, unfairness or missing resources can interrupt the cycle.

1. The loop most leaders never see

People are more likely to try when they believe effort can lead to progress and when the goal matters to them. Practice, useful feedback and reinforcement can help them improve; visible improvement can then strengthen confidence and willingness to try again. This is a practical synthesis of motivation and learning research, not a single universal formula.

When this cycle is supported, teams can build capability and confidence over time. When it breaks, disengagement may follow, and a bonus alone may not address the cause.

2. First question: is it motivation or ability?

Before treating a problem as low motivation, check whether people have the ability, training, tools, time and authority they need. A useful diagnostic is that performance depends on capacity, willingness and the opportunity to perform (Blumberg & Pringle, 1982). It is a practical lens, not a mathematical law. A pep talk cannot compensate for missing training or tools.

Motivation is often described in terms of direction (what someone is trying to do), intensity (how much effort they invest) and persistence (how long they sustain it) (Pinder, 2008). A useful diagnosis asks which of these, if any, is constrained.

3. Four places the loop breaks

Motivation theories come in two families. Content theories, such as Maslow's (1943) hierarchy, Alderfer's (1969) ERG theory, McClelland's (1961) needs theory and Herzberg's two-factor theory (Herzberg et al., 1959), explain what people need. Process theories explain how people decide whether to put in the effort. The first tells you what to offer. The second helps you see why it isn't working.

Vroom's (1964) expectancy theory is a useful process map: motivation depends on expectancy (effort can lead to performance), instrumentality (performance can lead to outcomes) and valence (those outcomes matter). The model is often expressed multiplicatively, so a very weak link in any one component can substantially weaken motivation. Adding fairness gives us four practical break points.

Break 1: "I can't do it." Expectancy is weak. Bandura (1977) identified four main sources of self-efficacy information: mastery experiences, observing others, verbal persuasion and physiological or emotional states. Mastery experiences are often especially influential. Leader expectations can also affect performance: in a field study of military trainees, raising instructors' expectations was followed by better trainee performance, an example of the Pygmalion effect (Eden & Shani, 1982). Expectations help; they don't guarantee results.

Break 2: "It's not worth it." Valence is weak: the outcome does not match what the person values. McClelland (1961) offers one useful lens: people may differ in the importance they place on achievement, influence and affiliation. Ask what matters to the person instead of assuming.

Break 3: "No one will notice." Instrumentality is weak. When strong and average work receive the same response, or when performance criteria are unclear, employees may struggle to see a reliable connection between effort, performance and outcomes.

Break 4: "It isn't fair." Adams (1963) described how people compare their contributions and outcomes with those of others. Organizational-justice research distinguishes fairness of outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment and information (Colquitt, 2001). In Lattice's 2024 survey, companies committed to increasing compensation transparency reported much higher employee engagement (72% vs. 39%); this is a reported association, not proof that transparency alone caused the difference (Lattice, 2024). Perceived breaches of the psychological contract can also undermine trust and commitment (Rousseau, 1995).

4. Goals, and the job itself

Specific, challenging goals can support performance when people are committed to them and receive feedback (Locke & Latham, 2002). For unfamiliar or complex work, pair outcome goals with learning goals that build capability. Break longer goals into shorter milestones so progress is visible. McKinsey's 2024 survey also found that employees reported greater motivation when goals were measurable, linked to organizational priorities and shaped with employee involvement (McKinsey & Company, 2024).

The job itself is another lever. Hackman and Oldham's (1976) Job Characteristics Model identifies skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy and feedback. Redesigning one or more of these features, or giving employees appropriate room to shape aspects of their role, may improve motivation without requiring a pay increase.

5. How learning scales: person, team, organisation

Individual learning can be understood as a cycle of experience, reflection, sense-making and experimentation (Kolb, 1984). Adult-learning theory also emphasizes self-direction and learning connected to real problems (Knowles et al., 2011).

Teams can develop transactive memory: a shared understanding of who knows what (Wegner, 1987). That makes knowledge continuity, documentation and cross-training important when a key person leaves.

Organisations can create knowledge by moving between tacit knowledge (experience-based know-how) and explicit knowledge (knowledge that can be documented), through processes such as sharing experience, articulating insights, combining documented information and applying what has been learned (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). Without deliberate knowledge-sharing practices, important lessons may leave with departing employees.

Organisations also learn at three depths (Argyris & Schön, 1978). "We missed the deadline, so let's work faster" is single-loop learning: fix the error. "Why do we keep setting deadlines we can't meet?" is double-loop: question the assumption. "Why did it take three missed deadlines before anyone asked?" is learning how to learn.

Success can conceal weaknesses when teams do not examine what drove it. Paradox research highlights how successful practices can create tensions or blind spots over time (Cunha & Putnam, 2019). Debrief wins as well as failures.

6. Myth vs evidence

Myth: "Money doesn't motivate people." Evidence: pay is an important motivator, and employees may understate its importance in surveys (Rynes et al., 2004). Its importance varies by person and context; fair, competitive pay matters, but it is not the only factor shaping motivation.

Myth: "Needs follow a strict ladder." Evidence: research has not consistently supported a rigid universal hierarchy of needs (Wahba & Bridwell, 1976). Alderfer's (1969) ERG theory allows different categories of needs to operate without requiring lower-level needs to be fully satisfied first.

Myth: "Teach people in their learning style." Evidence: research has not found adequate support for routinely matching instruction to labels such as "visual" or "auditory" learners (Pashler et al., 2008). Consider learner preferences, but choose methods based on the skill, task and evidence for effective instruction.

7. A 2-minute team check-up

Answer yes or no for your team:

  1. Does each person know what "great" looks like in their role?
  2. Has each person had a visible win in the last 30 days?
  3. Do people get specific feedback within a week of the work?
  4. Do you know what each person values most: money, growth, flexibility or recognition?
  5. Is good work recognised differently from average work?
  6. Would your team say workload and rewards are fair?
  7. Have you kept the promises you made at hiring and at the last review?
  8. Is there room to try something new without being punished for a first mistake?

Questions 1–3 test clarity, progress and feedback. Question 4 explores individual values. Question 5 checks whether effort and contribution are recognised. Questions 6–7 examine fairness and trust. Question 8 explores whether people can learn safely. Every "no" points to an area worth investigating, not proof of a single cause.

8. The 30-day playbook

Week 1, ask, don't assume. In every 1:1, ask: "What would make this work more worth your effort?" Write the answers down.

Week 2, set one learning goal with each person: a skill to build, not a number to hit.

Week 3, engineer a small win. Give each person a stretch task they can finish within a week, then recognise it specifically: what they did, and why it mattered.

Week 4, review fairness and follow-through. Look at workload, recognition and promises made, and fix one practical issue. Then debrief a recent win with the team: what worked, what conditions helped, and what should be repeated?

The bottom line

You can't install motivation in anyone.

But you can stop breaking the loop that creates it.

Learning can build belief. Belief can strengthen effort. A leader's job is to create conditions that keep the loop moving.

Which of the eight check-up questions would your team answer "no" to first?
