Most leaders lose influence without noticing the moment it happens.

There are no blow-up and no resignation letter. Meetings still run, decisions still get made, and people still say "sounds good." What disappears is quieter: the pushback that would have saved a bad decision, the early warning about a client, the idea someone decided wasn't worth the risk of saying out loud.

Over the past two weeks, I've written about power, conflict and communication as separate posts. In practice, no leader experiences them separately. They form one triangle, and influence lives in the middle of it.

1. The Influence Triangle

Picture three corners.

  • Power is your right to be heard: where your influence comes from.
  • Conflict is the stress test: what happens when people disagree with you.
  • Communication is the channel: what actually reaches people when you speak, and what reaches you when they do.

In the centre sits voice: whether people tell you the truth. Every corner feeds it, and any weak corner drains it. A leader with deep expertise but poor listening loses voice. A great communicator who punishes disagreement loses voice. Influence is only as strong as the weakest corner.

2. A 60-second diagnosis

When influence stalls, leaders usually blame the people ("they're not engaged") or the message ("I need to explain it better"). Before you do either, ask which corner is failing.

  • If people do what you ask but never more, look at power. You may be running on position, not trust.
  • If disagreements go underground or turn personal, look at conflict.
  • If people act on something different from what you meant, look at communication.
  • If bad news keeps surprising you, more than one corner is probably leaking into the centre.

Fix the corner you diagnosed, not the one you're most comfortable fixing.

3. Power: the corner that goes deaf

Gary Yukl describes power as a capacity to influence others; influence is the change that actually happens. And power can change the person who holds it. Research by Dacher Keltner and colleagues suggests that feeling powerful makes people more action-focused and less attentive to others' perspectives. In one well-known experiment, Adam Galinsky and colleagues asked people to draw the letter E on their own forehead. Those primed to feel powerful were more likely to draw it the right way round for themselves, and backwards for anyone looking at them.

The lesson isn't that power is bad. It's that power can quietly reduce the input you receive just when your decisions matter most.

The way back runs through how you influence. A meta-analysis by Higgins, Judge and Ferris found that "soft" tactics, such as rational persuasion and consultation, tend to be more effective than "hard" ones such as pressure. Yukl and Tracey found rational persuasion, inspirational appeals and consultation were the tactics most likely to earn commitment.

Sharing power goes further. Gretchen Spreitzer describes psychological empowerment as four beliefs: the work has meaning, I'm competent, I have a choice, and I have an impact. A 2024 meta-analysis by Llorente-Alonso and colleagues links empowerment to higher job satisfaction, commitment and performance.

4. Conflict: the disagreement you never hear

Unequal power changes how conflict shows up. Morton Deutsch described how power imbalance shapes conflict, and Folger and Cropanzano note that the less powerful side often avoids or accommodates rather than pushing back. The conflict doesn't disappear. It goes underground, as resentment, quiet resistance and ideas nobody voices.

Culture can deepen this. In high power-distance cultures, as Geert Hofstede described them, openly disagreeing with a senior person can feel disrespectful, so conflict goes quiet rather than away. Leaders of multicultural teams, which in Canada is most of us, need to make disagreement explicitly safe, not just technically allowed.

When disagreement does surface, aim for an integrative solution rather than splitting a fixed pie. Fisher, Ury and Patton's principled negotiation offers four moves that work as well in a team meeting as in a boardroom: separate the people from the problem; focus on interests, not positions; generate options before deciding; and agree on objective criteria. A position is "the deadline is the 15th." An interest is "the client's board meets on the 18th." Interests leave room to move.

5. Communication: the silence you don't hear

The most expensive communication failure isn't what leaders say badly. It's what employees never say. In interviews by Frances Milliken and colleagues, 34 of 40 professionals (85%) said that, at least once, they had felt unable to raise an important issue at work. Fear of being labelled negatively and of damaging a relationship were the most common reasons.

James Detert and Amy Edmondson found that people carry "implicit voice theories": unwritten rules such as "don't embarrass the boss in public" or "have a solution before you raise a problem." An open-door policy doesn't override those rules. A leader's behaviour can. Edmondson's research on psychological safety shows that teams engage in more learning behaviour when people believe they won't be punished for speaking up.

When you're the one speaking, close the loop. Communication texts such as Kaul's treat feedback as the step where shared meaning is confirmed, so ask for a playback rather than "Any questions?"

Upward communication has to be designed. It can't just be hoped for.

6. Myth vs evidence

  • Myth: "93% of communication is nonverbal." Evidence: Albert Mehrabian's studies looked at single words spoken with conflicting tone and facial expression, in a lab, and Mehrabian never claimed the figure applies to everyday conversation. Nonverbal cues do matter, especially when they contradict what you say (Bonaccio and colleagues). Words still carry the content.
  • Myth: "Good teams don't have conflict." Evidence: in Allen Amason's research on top management teams, debate about the issues was linked to better decisions, while personal friction worked against them.
  • Myth: "Power is about control." Evidence: durable influence tends to come from expertise and trust, and sharing power through empowerment is linked to higher commitment and performance.
  • Myth: "My door is always open, so people will tell me." Evidence: silence is driven by perceived risk, not by whether the door is open.

7. Team check-up: eight questions

Answer honestly, then ask your team to answer them about you.

  1. When I make a request, do people suggest improvements, or only comply?
  2. Have I changed a decision because of someone's input in the last month?
  3. Do I consult people before decisions that affect them?
  4. When we disagree, do we stay on the issue rather than on personalities?
  5. Do I know the interests behind the positions people take on my team?
  6. Do I check understanding by asking for a playback, rather than "Any questions?"
  7. Has someone junior brought me bad news early in the past month?
  8. Does anyone on my team disagree with me in public, and is that safe?

Every "no" points to a corner of the triangle worth investigating.

8. The 30-day playbook

  • Week 1, Map: list the decisions coming up and who they affect. Note where you're relying on your title.
  • Week 2, Consult: bring one decision to the team before it's final. Ask "What am I missing?" and act on one answer, visibly.
  • Week 3, Surface: pick one simmering disagreement. Name it as a task issue, ask each side for their interests, and agree on criteria.
  • Week 4, Close the loop: end every key meeting with "What will you do first?" and publicly thank one person for bad news delivered early.

The bottom line

Influence isn't something you have. It's something people decide to give you, one conversation at a time. Power gets you into the room, conflict tests whether you deserve to stay, and communication decides whether anything you say survives the walk back to people's desks.
