In 2003, the board investigating the loss of the space shuttle Columbia reached a conclusion that still unsettles leaders. NASA's organizational culture, it found, had as much to do with the accident as the piece of foam that struck the wing. Foam had come loose on earlier flights and had come to be treated as a normal, "in-family" event. Engineers asked for better images of the damaged wing; the requests went nowhere (Columbia Accident Investigation Board [CAIB], 2003).

No one set out to build that culture. Like every culture, it was built from thousands of small moments: a question not asked, a concern softened, a meeting that moved on.

This edition connects the two posts of this series. The first showed that teams turn at moments you can predict. The second showed that culture, more than pay, is often why people leave. Here is the toolkit that links them.

1. The culture ripple

Building on organizational culture research, I use four connected rings to explain how everyday leadership moments become culture. Think of them as the rings that spread when a stone hits water.

• Ring 1, Moments: what a leader does when it matters. What they ask about, how they react to bad news, what they reward. Schein (2017) calls these the primary embedding mechanisms.

• Ring 2, Norms: repeated moments harden into the team's unwritten rules, such as who speaks first and whether mistakes are discussed or hidden (Feldman, 1984).

• Ring 3, Climate: norms create how it feels to work here right now, including whether people feel safe to take interpersonal risks (Edmondson, 1999; Schneider et al., 2013). Climate is the team's shared sense of what gets rewarded, supported and expected. It can shift faster than culture.

• Ring 4, Culture: over time, climate settles into shared assumptions about "how we do things here", the organization's personality. It is the slowest ring to move (Schein, 2017).

The ripple runs both ways. Culture flows back into every new moment through who gets hired, who gets promoted and which stories get told.

2. The 60-second diagnosis: which ring is broken?

• Meetings feel fine but nothing changes? Look at Moments: what you pay attention to and follow up on.

• People say one thing in the meeting and another in the corridor? Look at Norms: the unwritten rules are winning.

• The same few voices dominate and bad news arrives late? Look at Climate: speaking up feels risky.

• The values on the wall don't match who gets promoted? Look at Culture: the say–do gap has become an assumption.

3. Moments: where culture is actually written

Schein's six primary mechanisms are a mirror for any leader: what you pay attention to, measure and control; how you react to crises; how you allocate scarce resources; what you deliberately role-model and teach; how you reward; and how you recruit, promote and remove people. Structures, procedures, rituals and formal statements matter too, but Schein treats them as secondary. They reinforce a culture when the primary signals agree with them, and they tend to be ignored when they don't (Schein, 2017).

A practical test: look at your last month of calendar entries and decisions. If someone inferred your values from those alone, what would they conclude?

4. Climate: safety is designed, not declared

Psychological safety, the shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking (Edmondson, 1999), is often treated as a trait of a kind manager. The evidence suggests it is also a design outcome. A meta-analysis of 136 samples covering more than 22,000 people found that role clarity (.63) and peer support (.62) were linked to psychological safety at least as strongly as positive relationships with the leader (.44), using corrected correlations (Frazier et al., 2017). These are correlations, not proof of cause, and the role-clarity and peer-support estimates rest on a handful of studies. Still, the message is practical: clear roles and peers who back each other are part of the safety you're trying to build.

Safety is also not comfort. Edmondson (2012) pairs it with accountability: high safety with low standards produces a comfort zone; high safety with high standards produces a learning zone. Low safety with high standards produces anxiety. Low safety with low standards produces apathy.

5. Culture: strong isn't the same as right

Strong cultures bring a clear sense of purpose, commitment and pride. They can also bring pressure to conform, an inward focus and resistance to change (Robbins & Judge, 2019). Very cohesive groups can slide into groupthink, where the wish for harmony overrides a realistic look at the alternatives (Janis, 1972).

There is no single best culture to copy, either. A meta-analysis of 84 studies built on the Competing Values Framework (Quinn & Rohrbaugh, 1983) found that different culture types were linked to different outcomes. Collaborative "clan" cultures were most strongly linked to employee attitudes, and competitive "market" cultures to profit. Clan and market values also tended to appear together rather than as opposites (Hartnell et al., 2011). The question isn't "Which culture is best?" It's "Which culture fits what we're trying to do?"

6. Myth vs evidence

• Myth: culture lives in the values statement. Evidence: Schein ranks formal statements as a secondary mechanism. Attention, reactions and rewards teach first (Schein, 2017).

• Myth: psychological safety means lowering the bar. Evidence: safety without accountability is comfort; safety with accountability is learning (Edmondson, 2012).

• Myth: a strong culture is always an asset. Evidence: strength can harden into conformity and resistance to change (Robbins & Judge, 2019). Aim for strong and adaptive.

• Myth: hire for culture fit. Evidence: hire for fit on values and add on perspective. Janis (1972) linked groupthink to cohesive groups whose members share similar backgrounds and views.

7. Team check-up

Score each statement from 1 (rarely true) to 5 (almost always true).

This is a practical reflection tool, not a validated assessment.

1. If someone makes a mistake on this team, it isn't held against them.

2. Everyone knows their role and what others count on them for.

3. Bad news reaches the leader early, not late.

4. Our unwritten rules match our written values.

5. The people we promote are the people who live our values.

6. We regularly discuss how we work, not just what we deliver.

7. Newcomers learn our real norms within their first month.

8. We can disagree openly and still decide together.

• 32 to 40: protect what you have.

• 21 to 31: pick the lowest two items and start there.

• 20 or below: begin with Ring 1, your own moments.

8. The 30-day playbook: the BridgeOne SCORE framework

• Days 1–5, Surface the unwritten rules. Ask the team to list "how things really work here". Keep the helpful ones; retire one that isn't.

• Days 6–12, Clarify roles and responsibilities. Build a one-page map of who owns what and who counts on whom.

• Days 13–18, Own the moments that matter. Pick your next tough meeting or piece of bad news and decide in advance how you'll respond.

• Days 19–24, Reward the behaviours you preach. Publicly recognize one person for how they achieved a result, not just what they achieved.

• Days 25–30, Expand the circle of perspectives. Before the next hire or project, ask: "What perspective are we missing?"

The bottom line

Culture is a team sport. Everyone on the field shapes it, but the leader touches the ball most often. Every leader is already writing culture. The only choice is whether you write it on purpose.

Which ring is hardest to move in your organization: moments, norms, climate or culture?

If your team is growing or changing, the biggest improvement often starts with one honest conversation about how you really work.
