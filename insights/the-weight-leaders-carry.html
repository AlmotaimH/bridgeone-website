The Weight Leaders Carry

Six pressures that sit underneath the results, and what research says about carrying them without breaking.

Hossam AlmotaimSeptember 29, 2026, 6 min read

Leadership is usually discussed through its results: the turnaround, the target hit, the team that finally clicked.

What rarely gets discussed is what leaders carry while producing those results. The pressure doesn't show up in the quarterly report, yet it shapes every decision behind it.

Here are six weights that almost every leader carries at some point, and what the research suggests about carrying each one well.

1. Starting from zero trust

Every leader eventually walks into a room where nobody knows them, and nobody is waiting to be impressed. New country, new team, new systems. And one silent question from everyone in the room: why should we trust you?

The instinct is to prove competence, fast. But competence is only one part of trust. Mayer, Davis and Schoorman (1995) found that people judge trustworthiness on three things: ability, benevolence (do you care about my interests?) and integrity (do your actions match your words?). A new leader can show ability in a week. Benevolence and integrity take consistency: showing up the same way, day after day, until consistency becomes credibility.

How to carry it: spend the first weeks understanding the room before trying to win it. Who has been there longest? What broke before you arrived?

2. The hidden cost of "whatever it takes"

"Whatever it takes" sounds like dedication. Often it is a warning sign. Long hours, weekends absorbed by work, patience wearing thin. The line between pushing hard and running on empty disappears so gradually that most leaders don't notice when they cross it.

The World Health Organization (2019) classifies burnout as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition: it results from chronic workplace stress that hasn't been successfully managed. Maslach and Leiter (2016) describe its three dimensions: exhaustion, cynicism and a falling sense of effectiveness. In other words, burnout is often a design problem, not a personal failure.

How to carry it: treat your own capacity as part of the team's results. A leader with nothing left to give eventually has nothing left to lead with.

3. When the numbers don't come in

Every leader misses a target eventually. Sometimes the market shifts; sometimes the timing is wrong. The instinct is to explain, defend, or disappear into the data looking for someone to blame.

Edmondson (2011) argues that organizations learn from failure only when leaders make it safe to examine what went wrong, instead of hunting for the culprit. Leaders set that tone with their own misses first.

How to carry it: own the miss before anyone asks: "Here's what happened. Here's what we're adjusting. Here's what we'll do differently." A missed target is data. How you respond to it is character.

4. The financial pressure no one talks about

There is a version of financial stress that rarely comes up in leadership conversations: the gap between what a role expects you to spend (travel, entertaining clients, representing the brand) and what the role actually pays. Admitting it feels like admitting weakness, so it gets absorbed in silence.

But financial stress doesn't stay financial. Mullainathan and Shafir (2013) show that scarcity captures attention and reduces the mental "bandwidth" available for everything else. Decisions start to be made from scarcity instead of strategy.

How to carry it: have the uncomfortable conversation with whoever can change the structure, instead of carrying the gap alone. And separate self-worth from net worth: neither a title nor a salary defines your value.

5. The loneliest place in leadership

The higher a leader climbs, the smaller the circle becomes. Informal conversations stop including you. Strong performance can create distance before it earns respect. Friends and family get what's left of your energy.

This isn't rare. In a survey reported by Saporito (2012), half of the CEOs said they experienced loneliness in the role, and most of that group believed it hurt their performance.

How to carry it: don't dim your standards to fit in, but don't lead from a distance either. Explain the intent behind your intensity, build a small circle of peers outside your organization, and invest in the relationships that feel least natural. The people who resist a strong leader early often become their strongest advocates, once they understand the intent.

6. The turning point nobody sees coming

Every hard season eventually reaches a moment that changes everything. It usually isn't dramatic. It is a quiet decision to trust the process, even when no one else does yet.

Coutu (2002) describes resilient people as sharing three traits: they face reality as it is, they find meaning in hardship, and they improvise with what they have. That is what resilience looks like in leadership. It isn't the absence of stress or doubt. It is holding your compass steady when the noise says drop it.

How to carry it: keep doing the right thing consistently, even when it's unpopular. The team that once challenged every decision often becomes the team that defends those decisions to others.

For organizations: stop adding weight you can't see

Most of these pressures are designed into leadership roles, not chosen by the people in them. Organizations can lighten the load:

  • Give new leaders a real onboarding period before judging results.
  • Treat sustained 60-hour weeks as a design problem to fix, not a badge of commitment.
  • Review targets for realism, and reward honest reporting of misses.
  • Fund the expenses a role requires, instead of leaving leaders to absorb them.
  • Give senior leaders access to peer networks, coaching or mentoring.

The bottom line

The results are what people see. The weight is what leaders carry to produce them.

None of these pressures is a sign that something is wrong with you. They are signs that you are doing something hard. Name the weight, share it with the right people, and keep your compass steady. Doing the right thing rarely feels rewarding in the moment. It almost always is in the end.

Over to you: which of these six weights have you carried, and what helped you carry it? Your answer might be exactly what another reader needs.
